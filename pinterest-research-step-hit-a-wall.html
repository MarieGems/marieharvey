The Pinterest Research Step That Hit a Wall I Could Not Fix

← Back to Random Thoughts
Marie Harvey · November 7, 2026

An AI pipeline I run for Marie Glow Studio's Pinterest work hit a wall on October 3, 2026 that no amount of debugging on my end could fix: a research step kept returning an empty page because Pinterest's own site was crashing before it finished loading, not because of anything in my own code. Here is how I confirmed the problem was not mine to fix, and what I changed about the pipeline instead.

What was the research step supposed to do?

Pull related search terms from a live Pinterest search page to help plan that day's pins. It had failed three runs in a row, on October 1, 2, and 3, each time coming back with an empty result from the search page.

How did I confirm the problem was not a bug in my own code?

By reproducing it directly in a fresh browser session instead of only reading the failure log. The page returned a normal success response and every one of its background data calls succeeded, but Pinterest's own front-end code crashed partway through loading and left the page blank, even after a reload. That is a failure inside Pinterest's own site, confirmed on a live retest, a fourth failure in a row.

Was there any way to get the data without triggering the crash?

I tried a plain, direct fetch of the same page to see if the related-search data sat anywhere in the page's own underlying data before the crash happened. It did not. The related-search terms are only ever filled in by a separate step that runs after the page finishes loading in a browser, and that step never fires once the crash happens first. There was no code-side way around it.

What do you do with a step that genuinely cannot be fixed?

Stop paying its cost every day. The step had always been set up so a failure never held up the rest of the batch, so no pins were actually affected while this was happening. I changed the instructions so that once this specific step fails three or more runs in a row, the pipeline stops attempting it daily and instead checks back on it once a week, so it is not spending its retry budget every single day on something already confirmed broken.

How do you tell a resilience fix from a correctness fix?

By asking whether anything was actually broken in the output. Here, the batch that same day still published all ten of its pins, so there was nothing wrong to correct. The fix changes how often the pipeline wastes effort retrying a dead approach, not what it produces, which is why I think of it as a resilience fix rather than a correctness fix.

Why wait for three failed runs before changing anything?

Because one bad run can be a fluke, and changing a process on a single data point is how you end up guessing instead of knowing. Three failures in a row, each reproduced the same way, is enough to call a pattern instead of bad luck, which is why the new rule only kicks in after three or more consecutive failures, not after the first one.

The bottom line on a step you cannot fix

Confirm the failure is really outside your own code before you spend more time debugging your own side of it, and once you are sure, change how often you try instead of forcing a fix that does not exist.
