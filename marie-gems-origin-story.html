From Bullet Journals to DTF: How Marie Gems Actually Changed

← Back to Random Thoughts
Marie Harvey · September 2, 2026

Marie Gems didn't start as a DTF shop. If you'd told me three years ago that this business would end up centered on printing and pressing custom transfers, I'm not sure I would've believed you — because back then, the whole operation was stickers and bullet journals, made in a two-bedroom apartment with my family filling up every other inch of the space.

The early days: stickers and journals

Those first products were simple, low-overhead, and honestly a good way to learn the basics of running a shop — sourcing materials, pricing something for the first time, figuring out what actually sells versus what just sounded good as an idea. Nothing about it was glamorous. It was just the starting point.

The middle chapters: drinkware, apparel, tumblers, sublimation

From there, the product line kept shifting. Drinkware and tumblers came in, then apparel, then sublimation as a printing method. Each shift meant learning something new — new equipment, new techniques, new problems to solve. None of it happened because of some master plan. It happened because I kept paying attention to what was working and what wasn't, and I was willing to change direction instead of forcing a product line to keep going out of stubbornness.

Why DTF transfers became the focus

DTF — direct-to-film transfers — is where Marie Gems landed, and it's stayed the focus because of what it actually allows: transfers that work across a huge range of fabrics and colors, without the material restrictions that come with something like sublimation. That flexibility matters a lot when you're running a shop solo and don't have room to specialize in ten different processes at once.

And I run the equipment myself — the printer, the film, the ink, the powder, the heat press, all of it. No outsourcing to a print-on-demand service. That means every transfer that goes out the door has actually passed through my hands, start to finish.

Three years of trial and error

That's really what this whole evolution has been — trial and error, over and over, for three years straight. Bullet journals to tumblers to DTF isn't a straight line, and it wasn't supposed to be. It's what happens when you let a business keep becoming whatever actually works, instead of freezing it at whatever it was when you started.

If you're earlier in that process yourself — still figuring out what your shop actually wants to be — I'd tell you the same thing I'd tell myself three years ago: the pivot isn't the failure. Staying stuck on the wrong product line out of pride is.
