Why I Loosened My Blog's "I Use It" Verdict Rule Early

← Back to Random Thoughts
Marie Harvey · November 20, 2026

My own blog's honesty rule once cost me 6 of 15 topics in a single batch, and the fix was not to loosen the honesty, it was to loosen what I had assumed the rule actually required. On September 21, 2026, I looked at why the 2026-09-24 Business Owner Favorites batch had left 6 of its 15 topics unused, and the reason surprised me: the writer agent had read "write it in first person" as part of the honesty rule itself, when it was never meant to be.

What happened with the batch that left 6 topics unused?

The 2026-09-24 Business Owner Favorites batch needed posts like "Things I Bought for My Business That Were Actually Worth It" and "What I'd Buy Again for My Home Office," and both needed a real first-person verdict, an "I bought this" or "I love this," that no file on record actually confirmed. Rather than invent one, the writer agent left both topics unused, along with four more with the same problem.

Why did leaving them unused feel like the safe choice?

Because the honesty rule is real and non-negotiable: never assert an unconfirmed personal use or purchase. Six topics all shared the exact shape that rule was built to catch, a claim about my own experience that no source backs up. Leaving them unused looked like the rule working correctly.

What did I actually say when I saw the result?

That the first-person framing "was just a guideline," and to change it and "make whatever fits best." That one line separated two things that had been treated as one: the honesty requirement itself, which is not optional, and the first-person voice, which was only ever a style preference.

What is the honesty rule now, stated precisely?

Never assert an unconfirmed personal use or purchase. That is the whole rule. It does not say every post must be written in the first person, and it does not say a topic without a personal verdict has to sit unused. It says do not claim something about my own experience that is not actually confirmed anywhere.

So what happens to a topic that still lacks a personal verdict?

It gets re-angled instead of left behind. A buyer's guide, a sizing guide, a second-person how-to, or "on my list" language can all carry a topic that an "I bought this" framing cannot, without inventing anything. The product still has to be real and named, and the facts still have to be confirmed. Only the voice around them changes.

Did this change what counts as an invented fact?

No, and that line never moved. Real, named affiliate items and confirmed facts only, still no invented specs, purchases, or results. Loosening the framing rule did not loosen the honesty rule sitting underneath it; it just stopped the framing rule from quietly acting like a second honesty rule it was never meant to be.

The bottom line on loosening a rule I wrote myself

A rule that is stricter than it needs to be still costs you something, even when it is well-intentioned: six real topics I could have covered sat unused over a mistake in how I had read my own instructions. My small business supplies list, sorted by job and paper planning tools for a scatterbrained business owner are two of the posts from that same batch. Frequently asked questions

What is Marie Harvey's actual honesty rule for product posts?

Never assert an unconfirmed personal use or purchase. It does not require first-person writing and does not require a topic to stay unused without a personal verdict on file.

What happens to a topic with no personal verdict on file?

It gets re-angled as a buyer's guide, a sizing guide, a second-person how-to, or "on my list" language, using only real, named items and confirmed facts, never an invented purchase.
