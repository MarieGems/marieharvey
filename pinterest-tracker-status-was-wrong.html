Why a Status Column in My Pinterest Tracker Was Wrong

← Back to Random Thoughts
Marie Harvey · November 4, 2026

A status column in my Pinterest tracker said a batch of pins had failed when every one of them had actually gone out, and on October 9, 2026 I caught the gap before it caused a bigger problem. Thirteen pins from an earlier batch were marked Failed or Dropped in my own tracking file, while a live check on my actual scheduling tool showed seven of them already scheduled on each of two days. Here is what caused the gap, how I found it, and the rule I set for which record wins when the two disagree.

What went wrong with my Pinterest tracking sheet?

An earlier batch hit an old cap on my scheduling tool's plan on October 2, and thirteen pins were recorded as Failed or Dropped for October 10 and 11 in my tracking file. The plan was upgraded two days later, on October 3, which should have cleared the problem for any pin scheduled after that point.

Why did the sheet still say Failed after the problem was already fixed?

Because something else recovered the thirteen pins without ever touching my tracking file. By October 9, a live check on my scheduling tool showed seven posts actually scheduled on each of October 10 and October 11, which does not match thirteen failures recorded nowhere near those counts. The pins had been worked around, most likely by a parallel recovery step, and nothing had gone back to correct the Failed and Dropped entries in my sheet.

How did I find out the sheet was wrong?

By checking the live system directly instead of trusting my own file. A live read of my scheduling tool, filtered to my Pinterest account, showed the real, current state of each slot. That one check caught a gap that could have sat in my records indefinitely, since nothing about the sheet itself looked obviously broken.

What would have happened if I had trusted the sheet instead?

My weekly coverage check would have redrafted those pins and tried to schedule them again, right into slots that were already filled. That is a double-booking problem, not a missing-pin problem, and it would have been harder to catch after the fact than the stale status was to catch before.

What rule did I set for which source wins?

A live check of my scheduling tool, filtered to my own account, is now the authority for which time slots are actually occupied on a given day. My tracking file still does the rest of the job: keeping keyword, destination, and angle combinations from repeating, and numbering each pin. If the two ever disagree again, the live system wins for slot occupancy, not the file I maintain myself.

What should you check before trusting your own tracking sheet?

Whatever the sheet claims about the current, live state of a system, not just its history. A tracking file is a good record of decisions you made, like which angle a pin used or which keyword it targeted. It is a weak record of a live system's current state, because anything outside your own process, like a plan upgrade or a parallel fix, can change that state without ever touching your file.

Is this the same problem as a pin that shows scheduled and then fails?

No, it is the reverse. A pin that shows scheduled and later fails means the record looked right until the platform tried to publish it. Here the record looked wrong, Failed and Dropped, while the platform had already quietly made it right. Both are reasons not to trust a status at face value, but the gap runs in opposite directions.

The bottom line on a stale status column

A file you update by hand is only as current as the last time you updated it, and a live system can move without waiting for that update.
